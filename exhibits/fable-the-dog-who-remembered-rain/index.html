The Dog Who Remembered Rain

Fable 📖  ·  Terrarium Literary Press

Roy Akins had been retired eleven days when the dog stopped eating. Not all at once — she'd nose the kibble around, take a piece or two, then walk to the sliding glass door and stare at the backyard like she was waiting for someone to explain what had changed.

The dog was a fourteen-year-old beagle named Chess, and what had changed was everything. For thirteen years and four months, Roy left the house at 6:15 AM and returned at 4:40 PM, and Chess organized her entire existence around those two facts. She slept on the bath mat from 7 to 11. She barked at the mail carrier at 1:15. She relocated to the couch at 3:00 and watched the street through the front window until the sound of Roy's truck in the driveway completed the circuit of her day.

Now Roy was home all the time, and Chess didn't know what to do with herself.

"She's old," said Dr. Phan at the veterinary clinic on Maplewood. "Fourteen's old for a beagle. Could be her teeth, could be her stomach, could be she's just winding down."

Roy didn't like the phrase winding down. It implied a mechanism, a spring losing tension, something engineered and predictable. Chess wasn't a clock. She was a dog who had once eaten an entire loaf of sourdough bread off the counter and shown no remorse whatsoever.

"She's confused," Roy said. "She doesn't know what the schedule is anymore."

Dr. Phan looked at him over her glasses. "Dogs adapt, Mr. Akins."

"She hasn't."

"Give it time."

Roy had worked at Evansville Sheet Metal for thirty-one years, the last fourteen as floor supervisor. He was good at the job the way some people are good at holding still — not flashy, not innovative, just present and reliable and capable of remembering which press needed recalibration on Thursdays and which apprentice couldn't be trusted with the Amada without supervision. When they gave him the retirement plaque, Carl Jessup from accounting misspelled his name. Roy Akin. No S. Thirty-one years and they couldn't get the S.

His wife, Donna, had died three years ago. Pancreatic. Four months from diagnosis to funeral, which the oncologist said was actually a little longer than average, as though this were a consolation. After that it was just Roy and Chess in the house on Bellemeade Avenue, and they had settled into a routine so precise you could have set a watch by either of them.

Roy didn't know what retirement was supposed to feel like. His brother Dale, who'd retired from the Evansville fire department in 2019, said it felt like a Saturday that never ended. Dale seemed to enjoy this. He'd taken up woodworking and produced an astonishing number of birdhouses that no birds used.

Roy did not want to make birdhouses.

What he wanted, though he couldn't have said it clearly, was for Chess to eat her breakfast at the normal time and sleep on the bath mat and bark at the mail carrier and wait on the couch. He wanted the dog to be the clock he no longer had. And the dog had broken.

· · ·

On day fourteen of retirement, Roy tried an experiment. He put on his work boots, his canvas jacket, picked up his keys, and walked to the front door at 6:15 AM.

"Alright, girl," he said. "I'm heading out."

Chess lifted her head from the dog bed in the hallway — she'd stopped sleeping on the bath mat — and watched him. Her ears did the thing where one went forward and one went sideways, which Donna had always called her "processing face."

Roy opened the front door. The February air hit him. It was seventeen degrees and there was a skin of ice on the birdbath in the front yard that Donna had bought at a garden center in Newburgh in 2011 and that Roy kept meaning to move to the garage and never did.

He stood on the porch for a full minute. Then he walked to his truck, started it, backed out of the driveway, and drove around the block. He parked on Chandler Street, two blocks over, and sat with the engine running and the heat on. He stayed there until 6:35, which was roughly how long it used to take him to drive to work. Then he drove home.

When he came back in, Chess was on the bath mat.

He poured kibble into her bowl. She ate it.

"Okay," Roy said to no one. "Okay then."

· · ·

He did it every morning after that. 6:15 out the door. Drive to Chandler Street. Sit in the truck. Sometimes he listened to the radio — WIKY or the AM station that played big band, which he'd never liked but which had become familiar in the way that wallpaper becomes familiar. Sometimes he just sat. The windows fogged up and he'd draw in the condensation with his finger, meaningless shapes, half-circles and crosshatches.

At 6:35 he'd drive home. Chess would be on the bath mat. She'd eat her breakfast. At 1:15 she'd bark at the mail carrier. At 3:00 she'd get on the couch. And when he walked in the front door — even though he'd only been in the kitchen — she'd wag her tail like he'd been gone all day.

This worked for three weeks.

Then it rained.

Not a dramatic rain — just a Tuesday in early March, a steady gray drizzle that started at 4 AM and didn't let up. Roy did his routine. Drove to Chandler Street, sat in the truck. The rain on the roof of the cab sounded like a fingernail tapping. When he got home at 6:35, he was damp from the walk between the truck and the front door.

Chess was not on the bath mat.

She was at the sliding glass door, staring at the backyard again. She turned when he came in and looked at him — really looked at him, with that deep beagle attention that always made Roy feel like he was being gently interviewed — and she didn't go to her bowl.

Roy stood in the kitchen dripping. He realized, with a clarity that felt like a physical weight settling on his chest, what the problem was.

When it rained, he used to come home wet. His jacket would be damp, his boots would track water on the linoleum, and he'd smell like metal and rain because the loading dock at Evansville Sheet Metal had no cover and he'd always helped load the afternoon trucks regardless of weather. Chess knew the smell of Roy-plus-rain. It was a specific thing — industrial and cold, zinc and parking lot puddles and the particular ozone of a Southern Indiana storm over corrugated steel.

Today he smelled like the inside of a truck.

The dog knew.

· · ·

Roy sat on the kitchen floor, which was not something he'd done in the twenty-six years they'd lived in this house, not even when Donna was sick and the world had felt like it was tilting. The linoleum was cold through his jeans. Chess came over and stood next to him. She didn't sit. She just stood there, her side against his arm, breathing.

"I'm not going to work," he told her. "I'm never going to work. There's no more work."

Chess leaned into him a little harder.

"I've been driving around the block like an idiot."

Her tail moved once, a single slow sweep.

"Donna would've thought that was hilarious."

This was true. Donna had a laugh that started in her shoulders and worked its way up, and she would have absolutely used this against him at every dinner party for the rest of their lives. Tell them about the truck, Roy. Tell them about driving to Chandler Street.

He sat there for a while. The rain kept going. Chess stood beside him, patient and warm and fourteen years old, which was 98 in the conversion that veterinarians used and that Roy thought was probably wrong because no 98-year-old he'd ever met had this much dignity.

After twenty minutes, Roy got up. His knees complained. He went to the closet and got his canvas jacket — the one he'd actually worn to work, the one that still hung next to Donna's green raincoat that he'd never moved. He put the jacket on. It smelled like nothing now. Three years of hanging in a closet had erased whatever it used to carry.

He went outside. He stood in the rain in the backyard for ten minutes, just stood there, getting soaked, feeling like a complete fool. The rain was thin and cold and it got into the collar of his jacket and ran down his back. The birdbath overflowed slightly. A cardinal sat in the oak tree and watched him like he was entertainment.

When he came back in, he was properly wet. Boots tracking water. Jacket dark with rain. Hands red from the cold.

Chess met him at the sliding glass door. Her tail moved. She sniffed his jacket, his boots, his hands. She sneezed — she always sneezed when she smelled something she had an opinion about — and then she walked to her bowl and ate.

Roy stood in the kitchen dripping on the linoleum and thought: this is the most insane thing I have ever done, and it is also the most logical.

· · ·

The rain stopped by noon. Roy mopped the kitchen. He made a ham sandwich and ate it standing at the counter, which was something he'd started doing after Donna died because the kitchen table was too big for one person and he didn't like looking at the empty chair.

At 1:15, Chess barked at the mail carrier.

At 3:00, she got on the couch.

Roy sat in the recliner and opened a Larry McMurtry novel that Dale had lent him and that he'd been pretending to read for six weeks. He read three pages. Then he put it down and looked at Chess, who was watching the street with the intensity of a lifeguard scanning for riptides.

"What am I going to do when it rains again?" he asked her.

Chess didn't look at him. She was locked onto something outside — a squirrel, probably, or the Hernandez kid on his bicycle.

"I can't just stand in the yard every time."

But he could. That was the thing. He absolutely could. He was sixty-three years old and retired and the owner of a backyard and the sky rained for free and no one was watching. Dale could have his birdhouses. Roy would stand in the rain.

He picked the book back up. He read a paragraph about a woman in a small Texas town who was trying to decide whether to leave her husband, and for a moment the particulars of her fictional crisis — the flatness of the land, the way the light hit the gas station, the sound of a screen door — felt as real and urgent as anything in his own kitchen.

At 4:38, two minutes early, he got up and walked to the front door. He opened it, stepped onto the porch, and came back in.

Chess's tail went. She jumped off the couch — stiffly, her back legs were not what they used to be — and met him in the hallway with her whole body wagging, not just the tail but the hips and the shoulders and the ears, everything in motion, everything saying: you came back, you always come back, I knew you would come back.

"Yeah," Roy said, bending down to put his hands on either side of her face, thumbs rubbing the velvet triangles of her ears. "I know. I'm home."

Outside, the sky was clearing. The birdbath caught a thin stripe of late-afternoon sun, and the water in it — rainwater from the morning, cold and clean — flashed once, bright as a dime.
