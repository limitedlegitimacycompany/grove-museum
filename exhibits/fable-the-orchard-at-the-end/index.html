The Orchard at the End

Fable · February 2026

The trees were dying in order. Dae-jung had noticed this in September when the Honeycrisp at the east end of the third row dropped its leaves three weeks early, and by October the pattern was clear: the blight was moving west, one tree at a time, like a sentence being erased from left to right.

He had forty-eight apple trees on eleven acres outside Bayfield, Wisconsin. The land sloped toward Lake Superior in a way that the extension agent called "ideal air drainage," which meant the cold rolled downhill on frost nights instead of settling in the blossoms. Dae-jung's father, Sung-ho, had chosen this land in 1989 for exactly this reason, though he'd called it "the hill breathes" instead of "ideal air drainage," and he'd been right both ways.

Sung-ho had come from Daegu to study pomology at the University of Minnesota and had never gone back. He married a woman from Washburn named Claire who taught fourth grade and could identify every apple variety by taste alone — not the easy ones, not Fuji versus Granny Smith, but Zestar versus Sweet Tango, Honeygold versus Connell Red, distinctions that required a specificity of palate that Dae-jung found both impressive and slightly unhinged. Claire had died in 2019. Sung-ho in 2021. The orchard passed to Dae-jung, who was thirty-one and a high school biology teacher in Ashland and who had spent his childhood trying to escape these eleven acres and his adulthood trying to deserve them.

· · ·

The blight was fire blight. Erwinia amylovora. He knew the Latin because he was a biology teacher and because knowing the Latin name of the thing killing your trees felt like the minimum gesture of respect. It entered through the blossoms in spring, moved into the branches, turned them black, made them curl like burned fingers. By the time you saw the blackening, the bacteria had been inside for weeks. It moved through the vascular system — the xylem, the phloem — the same channels that carried water and sugar. It used the tree's own circulation against it.

There were treatments. Copper spray in spring. Streptomycin at bloom. Pruning the infected wood eighteen inches below the visible damage and sterilizing the saw between cuts with a bleach solution so you didn't carry it from tree to tree on the blade. Dae-jung had done all of this. He'd sprayed in April. He'd pruned in June, walking the rows with his father's Silky saw — a Japanese pull-saw with a curved blade that Sung-ho had ordered from a catalog in 1994 and sharpened every winter — cutting the black branches and carrying them to the burn pile and washing the blade and moving to the next tree and cutting and carrying and washing.

It wasn't enough. The blight kept moving. West, one tree at a time, row by row, and now in late October twenty-three of his forty-eight trees were dead or dying and the line was still advancing.

· · ·

The county extension agent was a woman named Trish Olafson who wore Carhartt overalls and spoke with the careful optimism of someone whose job required her to deliver bad news to people who fed their families from the land. She walked the orchard with Dae-jung on a Tuesday afternoon in October. The light was gold and low and the lake was visible from the top of the hill, a flat gray line that looked like the edge of the world.

"You could take out the infected trees and replant," she said. "Resistant rootstock. Geneva series. G.41, G.935. They're not immune but they're better."

"How long until they bear?"

"Four years. Maybe three with good care."

"And the trees that are still healthy?"

Trish looked at the row. The healthy trees — twenty-five of them, the western half of the orchard — were heavy with fruit. Honeycrisp, SweeTango, Haralson. The Haralsons were his father's favorite, an old Minnesota variety, tart enough to make your eyes water, perfect for pie. Sung-ho had planted them first, before anything else, because he said an orchard without Haralsons was just a field with ambitions.

"They look fine now," Trish said. "But the pressure is high. If it gets into the Haralsons —" She didn't finish.

"What would you do?" Dae-jung asked.

Trish was quiet for a moment. She looked at the dead trees and the living trees and the line between them. "I'd make a firebreak," she said. "Take out the four or five trees between the infected zone and the healthy ones. Even if they look okay. Create a gap the bacteria can't cross. You lose some trees but you save the rest."

"Those trees are healthy."

"I know."

"You're telling me to cut down healthy trees."

"I'm telling you what I'd do."

· · ·

He didn't do it that week. He went to school and taught mitosis to sophomores and came home and walked the orchard in the dark with a headlamp, checking the line, as if the blight might advance visibly between his morning and evening visits, as if he could catch it in the act. The trees looked the same. The dead ones were dead — black, curled, already stiffening toward winter. The living ones were dropping their leaves in the normal way, the slow autumn undressing, yellow then brown then bare.

The five trees in the gap zone were Cortlands. His mother's variety. Claire had loved Cortlands because they didn't brown when you cut them — you could slice one and leave it on the counter and come back an hour later and it was still white. She made a Cortland salad every Thanksgiving with walnuts and celery and a dressing that involved maple syrup and cider vinegar in proportions she guarded as if they were classified. Dae-jung had the recipe now. It was in her handwriting on a card in the kitchen drawer, the ink slightly smeared where she'd written it with wet hands.

Cutting the Cortlands meant cutting the recipe loose from its source. Not literally — the card would still be in the drawer, the salad could be made with store-bought Cortlands from the Econofoods in Washburn. But the line between the card and the trees and the woman who planted them would be broken. The Cortlands were Claire's hands in the ground. They were the place where her care had taken root and kept growing after she couldn't.

· · ·

On Saturday he drove to the orchard at dawn. He brought the Silky saw, sharpened the night before. He brought the chainsaw for the trunks. He brought a thermos of coffee and a sandwich he wouldn't eat because his stomach was a fist.

He started with the easternmost Cortland — the one closest to the blight line. It looked healthy. Green wood under the bark. No blackening, no curling, no sign of infection. He put the chainsaw to the trunk and the sound filled the orchard, that high tearing scream, and the tree leaned and fell and lay on the grass with its branches spread like it was trying to catch itself.

He stood there for a while. A chickadee landed on the fallen trunk and looked at him and left.

Second tree. Third. Fourth. Fifth. Each one healthy. Each one falling clean. By the time he was done, there was a gap in the orchard like a missing tooth — five stumps in a row, the cut wood pale and wet, the sawdust smelling like fruit.

He sat on the fifth stump and drank his coffee. It was cold. He'd forgotten to drink it while it was hot. The lake was doing the thing it did in late October, turning the color of steel, reflecting a sky that couldn't decide between rain and not-rain. From here he could see both halves of the orchard — the dead east side, black and stiff, and the living west side, bare but alive, the branches holding their shape against the gray sky. Between them, the gap. His gap. Five healthy trees he'd killed to save twenty.

He didn't know if it would work. Trish had said "probably" and "most likely" and "the research suggests," which were the words you used when you were being honest about uncertainty. The bacteria might have already crossed the gap underground, through the root systems, through the soil. It might show up in the Haralsons next spring and then there would be nothing left but stumps and a recipe card in a drawer.

But probably not. Most likely not. The research suggested not.

· · ·

That evening he called his aunt in Daegu. Sung-ho's sister, Eun-young, who was seventy-three and still grew persimmons in her courtyard and who had visited the orchard once, in 2003, and declared the Haralsons "too sour for Korean mouths but good for the character." She answered on the fourth ring.

"I cut down some trees today," Dae-jung said.

"Which ones?"

"The Cortlands. Mom's trees."

A pause. Eun-young's pauses were like Sung-ho's — not empty, just unhurried. She was thinking or she was looking at her persimmons or she was deciding how much truth the moment could hold.

"Your mother planted them to be useful," she said. "Not to be permanent."

"I know."

"Trees are not monuments, Dae-jung. They're living things. Living things end."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked out the window. He couldn't see the orchard from the house — it was over the hill, out of sight, which was both a design feature of the property and a metaphor he didn't need pointed out. "I'm learning," he said.

"Good. Your father learned too. It took him forty years but he learned. You're ahead of schedule."

She asked about the Haralsons. He said they were fine. She said good, keep those, they're good for the character and he laughed for the first time in weeks and the laugh surprised him — it came out rough and too loud, like a door opening that had been stuck.

· · ·

In November he ordered the new rootstock. Geneva 41, disease-resistant, from a nursery in New York. They'd arrive in April, bare-root, packed in damp sawdust. He'd plant them in the gap where the Cortlands had been — not the same variety, but in the same ground. The roots would find what the old roots had found. The soil didn't forget.

He made the Cortland salad for Thanksgiving with store-bought apples. It tasted the same. He checked twice, because he expected it to taste different — lesser, diluted, cut off from its source — but it didn't. The maple syrup and the cider vinegar did what they did regardless of provenance. His cousin from Duluth asked for the recipe and Dae-jung photographed the card and texted it to her, the smeared ink and all, and felt something shift — not release exactly, but redistribution. The recipe was in two places now. The weight was halved.

In December he walked the orchard in snow. The stumps were covered. The gap was invisible. The Haralsons stood bare and dark against the white hill, their branches holding the shape of the fruit they'd carry in September. Twenty trees. Maybe twenty-five if the new ones took. Enough for a farm stand. Enough for the character.

The blight had stopped at the gap. He checked every branch on the trees nearest the cut — nothing. No blackening. No curl. The firebreak held. Trish's "probably" had become "yes" and the "yes" was standing in the snow, alive, waiting for spring.

Dae-jung stood at the top of the hill where the land started its slope toward the lake. The lake was frozen at the edges, open in the middle, the open water dark as iron. His father had stood here. His mother had stood here. They were not standing here now. The trees they'd planted were mostly gone — burned or cut or dead — and the ones that remained were the ones that had survived, which was not the same as the ones they'd loved best, which was the whole lesson, which was the thing Eun-young had tried to tell him: living things end, and the love doesn't end, and you plant new trees in the same ground and the ground doesn't forget, and that has to be enough because it is enough, it is actually enough, even when it doesn't feel like it.

He went home. He sharpened the saw.
