The Empty Room

Fable · February 2026

The apartment had one room that Seo-yun couldn't fill. It was the second bedroom — nine by eleven, one window facing the courtyard, carpet the color of oatmeal. She'd moved in four months ago and the room was still empty. Not unfurnished. Empty. Nothing in it. Not a box, not a chair, not a thing leaning against the wall waiting to be dealt with. Just the carpet and the window and the closet with its single wooden hanger left behind by whoever lived here before.

The rest of the apartment was full. Too full probably. The living room had the couch from the old place and the bookshelf from grad school and the coffee table she'd bought at the Salvation Army in Providence when she was twenty-three, the one with the ring stain that she'd stopped seeing years ago. The kitchen had every pan and utensil she'd ever owned, including duplicates — two vegetable peelers, three wooden spoons, a garlic press she'd never used but couldn't discard because her roommate Vanya had given it to her as a joke and the joke still lived in the object. The bedroom had her bed, her dresser, the reading chair with the broken arm that she propped up with a dictionary.

But the second bedroom was empty and she couldn't fill it and she didn't know why.

· · ·

Seo-yun was twenty-nine and had just moved to Rochester, Minnesota for a job she'd taken because it was offered and not because she wanted to live in Rochester, Minnesota. She was a biostatistician at the Mayo Clinic, which meant she spent her days with data sets — other people's blood work, other people's outcomes, columns of numbers that described what happened to bodies over time. She was good at this. She had a PhD from Brown and a particular talent for finding patterns in longitudinal data, for seeing the shape that a thousand individual trajectories made when you stepped back far enough. Close up, a patient was a person. From where Seo-yun sat, a patient was a point in a cloud, and the cloud had a shape, and the shape had meaning.

She'd moved from Providence, where she'd lived for seven years — five in grad school, two as a postdoc. Providence was small and human-scaled and full of students and the particular energy of a city that was always being discovered by twenty-two-year-olds who thought they'd invented it. Seo-yun had been one of those twenty-two-year-olds. Then she'd been twenty-five, then twenty-eight, and the city hadn't changed but she had, and by the end she felt like furniture in someone else's apartment — present, functional, belonging to a context that had moved on without her.

Rochester was different. Rochester was a hospital that had grown a city around it. The Mayo Clinic was the gravitational center and everything else — the restaurants, the apartments, the shops on Peace Plaza — orbited it with the quiet dependency of moons. People came to Rochester to be healed or to heal and there wasn't much in between. Seo-yun was in between. She wasn't sick and she wasn't a doctor. She was the person who looked at what happened to the sick people after the doctors were done and tried to find the pattern.

· · ·

Her mother, Ji-hye, called every Sunday from Ellicott City, Maryland, where she'd lived since Seo-yun was four. Ji-hye was a piano teacher. She taught from the house — a Yamaha upright in the living room, students arriving every forty-five minutes, the sound of scales drifting through the walls like weather. Seo-yun had grown up in that sound. She hadn't learned piano herself, which was either an act of rebellion or a failure of interest and which one it was depended on who was telling the story.

"How's the apartment?" Ji-hye asked.

"Fine."

"Did you set up the other room?"

"Not yet."

"What are you going to do with it?"

"I don't know."

"You could make it an office."

"I work at work."

"A guest room."

"No one's visiting."

"I could visit."

"Then I'll set it up before you come."

Ji-hye made the sound she made when she was deciding whether to push. It was the same sound she made when a student played a wrong note — a small intake of breath, a recalibration. She didn't push. She asked about the weather. Seo-yun said it was cold. Ji-hye said Maryland was also cold. They discussed cold for a while, which was easier than discussing the room.

· · ·

The thing about the room was that it wasn't a problem. Seo-yun had looked at it as a problem — what to put in it, how to use it, what it should become — and the problem had no solution because it wasn't a problem. It was a space. An empty space in a life that had been, until four months ago, entirely filled — filled with grad school and then the postdoc and then the job search and then the move. Every room in her life had been assigned a function. Study here. Sleep here. Eat here. The rooms were full because the schedule was full and the schedule was full because the plan was full and the plan had worked. PhD, postdoc, job. Checkboxes checked. A career trajectory that, from a distance, looked exactly like the upward curve in one of her data sets — steady, predictable, trending toward significance.

And now she was here, in the job, at the end of the plan, and there was a room with nothing in it.

She went in sometimes. Not often. Maybe twice a week, in the evenings, after dinner — she cooked for herself, simple things, rice and soup, the Korean food her mother made but simpler because she didn't have the patience for Ji-hye's level of banchan production and also because cooking for one was a different discipline than cooking for a family, a discipline of restraint, of making enough and not too much, of eating a meal that no one else had witnessed. After dinner she'd go to the room and stand in the doorway or sit on the carpet — the oatmeal carpet that had no stains, no wear pattern, no evidence of use — and look at the window.

The window faced the courtyard, which was a square of grass and a single tree — a maple that had dropped its leaves in October and now stood bare against the Minnesota sky, which in November was the color of aluminum. The tree was the only thing she could see from the empty room that was alive. Everything else — the brick walls of the other buildings, the concrete path, the dumpster enclosure — was material. Built. The tree was the exception. It stood in the courtyard doing the thing trees do in November, which is nothing, which is everything, which is surviving the withdrawal of light without complaint.

· · ·

At work she was analyzing a study on recovery times — how long it took patients to return to baseline after a specific cardiac procedure. The data had twelve hundred subjects and eighteen months of follow-up. Most patients recovered in a predictable curve: sharp improvement in weeks one through six, a plateau, then a gradual return to near-normal by month twelve. But there was a cluster — maybe eighty patients, six percent — whose recovery curves did something different. They plateaued early and stayed there. Not declining. Not improving. Just level. For months. And then, around month fourteen or fifteen, they started improving again, steeply, as if something had released.

Her PI, Dr. Lindgren, called it a delayed response. The body doing something invisible, reorganizing itself below the threshold of measurement, preparing for a recovery that didn't look like recovery until it was already happening.

"How do you distinguish delayed response from no response?" Seo-yun asked.

"You can't," Lindgren said. "Not until it moves. While it's flat, you don't know if it's gathering or done."

Seo-yun thought about this in the empty room that night. She sat on the carpet. The tree was dark against the darker sky. The window reflected her faintly — a woman sitting on the floor of an empty room in Rochester, Minnesota, thinking about flat lines and delayed responses.

She had been flat since she arrived. She had not made friends. She had not explored the city. She went to work and came home and cooked and ate and went to the empty room and sat and went to bed. This was not depression — she'd been depressed in her second year of grad school and this didn't feel like that. Depression was heavy. This was light. This was the absence of weight. She had arrived in Rochester without the plan that had carried her for seven years and she was floating in the space where the plan had been, the way an astronaut floats when the thrust stops — not falling, not rising, just drifting in the absence of force.

· · ·

In December the tree got ice. Not snow — ice. A freezing rain that coated every branch in glass, and in the morning when the sun came through the courtyard the tree looked like a chandelier, every twig lit, every surface reflecting. Seo-yun stood at the window in the empty room and watched it. The sun moved. The reflections moved. The ice didn't move. The tree held all of it — the weight, the light, the stillness — and didn't crack.

She stayed at the window for twenty minutes. She was late for work. She didn't care. She watched the tree hold the ice and the light move through the ice and the courtyard fill with something that was not warmth but was beauty, which was different from warmth but not less, and she felt the flat line in herself do something — not rise, not yet, but tilt. The smallest possible tilt. The kind of change that wouldn't show up in a data set. The kind that you couldn't distinguish from noise unless you were the one feeling it.

She called her mother that evening. Sunday was three days away but she called anyway.

"I think the room is going to stay empty," Seo-yun said.

"Why?"

"Because it's the only empty thing I have."

Ji-hye was quiet for a long time. The silence had the quality of her mother listening, really listening, the way she listened to a student play a piece for the first time — not judging, not correcting, just hearing what was there.

"Okay," Ji-hye said.

"Okay?"

"Okay."

Seo-yun looked at the room from the doorway. The carpet. The window. The one wooden hanger in the closet. The tree outside, dark now, the ice gone, the branches bare and fine against the sky. The room was empty and she was keeping it empty and this was not a failure to fill it but a decision to leave it open, the way a rest in music is not a failure to play but a choice to let the silence hold its shape.

Ji-hye would have understood this. Ji-hye, who spent her life teaching people to play, who knew that the rests mattered as much as the notes, who could hear the difference between a silence that was empty and a silence that was full. She'd said "okay" and she'd meant it and the okay was enough.

Seo-yun closed the door. The room stayed empty. She went to bed. Outside, the tree stood in the courtyard, bare, patient, waiting for whatever came next without needing to know what it was.
