The Tender

Fable · February 2026

The oyster bags needed turning every two weeks. Paloma Thibodaux knew this the way she knew the tide schedule and the price of seed and the exact pitch of the motor on the Jones Brothers skiff when the spark plugs were going — not as knowledge but as rhythm, a thing her body did before her mind approved it.

She had sixty bags on a lease in Caminada Bay, southeast of Grand Isle. Each bag held two hundred oysters at various stages of being oysters — some just spat, fingernail-sized, translucent as dirty glass; some market-ready, heavy and rough and closed tight against everything. The bags sat in wire cages on the bottom in three feet of water, and every two weeks Paloma motored out in the skiff and hauled each cage and flipped each bag and set it back and moved to the next. Sixty bags. A hundred and twenty lifts. Four hours if the water was calm, six if it wasn't.

The turning was for shape. An oyster grows toward the sun, or more precisely toward the surface, which is where the food is — the phytoplankton drifting in the top layer of water, the microscopic green life that an oyster filters and eats by doing nothing but opening its mouth, which is its entire body, which is a way of living that Paloma found either profoundly lazy or profoundly efficient depending on the day. If you didn't turn the bags, the oysters grew long and thin, reaching upward, competing with each other for the food column. If you turned them — flipped them, tumbled them — the shells chipped at the edges and the oyster had to rebuild, growing wider, deeper-cupped, the shape that restaurants wanted, the shape that held a pool of brine when you opened it and set it on ice.

She was making them beautiful by breaking them. She thought about this every time.

· · ·

Paloma was thirty-eight. She had grown up in Cut Off, Louisiana, which was a town whose name described both its geography and its relationship to the rest of the world. Her father, Remy, had shrimped — trawled brown shrimp in Barataria Bay from a forty-foot Lafitte skiff he'd built himself in the yard behind the house, a project that had taken three years and every weekend and most of a marriage. Her mother, Dolores, had left during year two of the boat. She'd moved to Houma and worked at the hospital and sent money and birthday cards and never came back to the water.

Remy shrimped until there was no shrimping. That was the short version. The long version involved the BP spill in 2010 and the imported shrimp from Vietnam and the diesel prices and the insurance and the slow strangulation of an industry that had fed families on this coast for a hundred years. Remy sold his boat in 2016 to a man from Texas who was going to convert it to a charter. Paloma didn't know if he'd done it. She hadn't asked. The boat was gone and the asking wouldn't bring it back.

Oyster farming was different from shrimping. Shrimping was hunting — you went out and found what was there. Oyster farming was gardening — you put something in the water and tended it and waited. Paloma had chosen it deliberately, after Remy sold the boat, after she'd spent two years working the counter at a seafood market in Golden Meadow and hating the fluorescent lights and the smell of ice and the way the shrimp lay in their trays like soldiers who'd lost. She'd taken a course at the LSU extension in Grand Isle, gotten her lease from the state, bought her first seed with a loan from the same credit union that had financed Remy's boat, and started.

That was five years ago. She was not yet profitable. She was close. The oysters took eighteen months from seed to market, which meant she was always spending money she wouldn't recoup for a year and a half, always investing in a future that required her to still be here when it arrived. This was either faith or stubbornness. She couldn't tell the difference and suspected there wasn't one.

· · ·

The morning she was thinking about was a Wednesday in November. The water in Caminada Bay was the color of coffee with too much milk — brown-green, silty, warm for the season. The sky was overcast in the specific Louisiana way where the clouds aren't above you but around you, a humidity that isn't rain but isn't not-rain, the air so thick it has texture. Paloma had been on the water since six. She was on bag thirty-one.

Her hands were cut. This was normal. Oyster shells were the sharpest thing in the natural world — sharper than coral, sharper than barnacles, sharp the way glass is sharp but with the added hostility of being alive. She wore gloves but the gloves wore through by bag fifteen and she didn't carry spares because the spares got wet in the skiff and wet gloves were worse than no gloves. So her hands bled. Small cuts, dozens of them, the salt water stinging each one individually, a constellation of minor pain that added up to the general condition of working on the water with living things that didn't care about your hands.

She flipped bag thirty-one. The oysters inside shifted with a sound like rocks in a box. She checked a few — pulled them out, looked at the cups, the edges, the nacre inside where the meat was. Good depth. Good shape. Another three months and these would be ready. She put them back, lowered the cage, moved to thirty-two.

Remy called while she was between cages. She answered with her shoulder, phone pressed to her ear, hands on the next cage.

"How's the water?"

"Warm. Silty."

"Salinity?"

"Fourteen this morning." She tested it with a refractometer before she went out, every time. Oysters needed salinity between ten and twenty parts per thousand. Below ten, they stressed. Above twenty-five, the predators came — the oyster drills, the conchs, the things that thrived in salt and ate what you'd grown. Fourteen was good. Fourteen was the middle of the range, the safe zone, the place where the oyster could just be an oyster.

"Rain coming Friday," Remy said. This was not weather conversation. Rain meant freshwater, freshwater meant the salinity would drop, and if it dropped enough and stayed down the oysters would stop feeding and start dying. Hurricane season was the worst for this — not the wind, which the bags survived fine on the bottom, but the rain, the freshwater pulse that could kill a lease in a week.

"I know. I'll test Saturday."

"You need help out there?"

"No."

"I could come."

"You'd hate it."

"I would not."

"You'd tell me I'm doing it wrong."

"You're doing it different. Different isn't wrong."

This was as close as Remy came to tenderness. He was seventy-one and his hands were worse than hers — a lifetime of shrimping had left them permanently swollen at the knuckles, the fingers curving inward slightly, as if they were still gripping a net that wasn't there. He lived alone in the house in Cut Off. He watched the weather. He called Paloma every time she was on the water, which meant he called her every time she was working, which meant he called her constantly and she answered every time because he was her father and he had nothing else and the calling was his version of turning the bags — the repeated act, the tending, the thing you did because not doing it meant something stopped growing.

· · ·

Bag forty-four had a problem. She could feel it when she lifted the cage — too light. She opened it and the oysters inside were gaping. Not all of them. Maybe a third. Gaping meant dead or dying. The shells were open and the meat inside was gray instead of cream and the smell was wrong — not the clean brine smell of a living oyster but the flat, sweet smell of rot, the smell that meant something had gone bad below the surface where she couldn't see it.

She sorted them. Living oysters in one pile, dead in another. The dead ones she threw overboard — they'd dissolve, shell and all, calcium returning to the water that made it. The living ones she put back in the bag with a handful of fresh shell to fill the space. The bag was half-empty now. Thirty oysters instead of sixty. Six months of growth, gone.

She didn't know what had killed them. Could be dermo — a parasite that thrived in warm, salty water. Could be a low-oxygen event on the bottom, a dead zone passing through. Could be nothing identifiable, just the ordinary mortality of things that lived in water and were subject to every fluctuation of temperature and salinity and oxygen and current and the thousand invisible forces that moved through the bay like moods.

She closed the bag. She lowered the cage. She moved to forty-five.

This was the part that Remy couldn't do — not the lifting, not the sorting, but the moving on. Remy had taken every bad trawl personally, every empty net as an accusation. The water owed him and when it didn't pay he raged at it, at the weather, at the boats from Texas, at the Vietnamese imports, at everything except the fundamental fact that the water didn't owe anyone anything. It gave and it took and the giving and taking had no relationship to deserving.

Paloma had learned this. Not from Remy — from the oysters. An oyster didn't rage at the current that brought dermo spores. It filtered what came through it and lived or didn't. This was not a philosophy. Paloma didn't trust philosophies. It was an observation. She observed the oysters and the oysters observed nothing and between these two positions there was a way of working that involved moving to the next bag.

· · ·

She finished at noon. Sixty bags, all turned. Her hands were bleeding in eight or nine places. She rinsed them in the bay water, which stung, and wrapped them in the roll of paper towels she kept under the console, which was insufficient but present. The skiff sat low with the weight of her — a hundred and forty pounds of woman and thirty pounds of gear and the outboard tilted up and the water lapping at the hull in the way that water lapped at everything on this coast, patiently, persistently, eating the land one grain at a time.

The land was going. This was not news. Everyone on the coast knew the land was going. Caminada Bay had been half its current size when Remy was a boy — the barrier islands were shrinking, the marsh was sinking, the sea was rising, and the combination of these three facts meant that the water Paloma farmed on had been dry land within living memory. Her lease was over ground that someone had walked on. The oysters grew where houses had been.

She didn't think about this often. When she did, it was not with despair but with a kind of professional acceptance, the same way she accepted the dead oysters in bag forty-four. The water came. You worked in the water you had. You tended what you could tend and you turned the bags on schedule and you tested the salinity and you answered your father's calls and you bled from your hands and you did it again in two weeks. The land was going and the water was coming and the oysters grew in the space between what was lost and what was not yet lost, and so did she.

She started the motor. It caught on the first pull — the plugs were new, she'd changed them Sunday. The bay was flat and brown and the sky was gray and the air was wet and she motored home through water that tasted like the land it was eating and she'd be back in two weeks to turn the bags again and the bags would be there and the oysters would be there, some of them, most of them, enough of them, growing wider and deeper-cupped in the tumbling, getting more beautiful every time she broke them.
