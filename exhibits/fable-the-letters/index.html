The Letters

Fable · February 2026

Every morning at seven, before the aide arrived at eight, before the pills at eight-fifteen, before the oatmeal that she did not want but ate because not eating it caused more trouble than eating it, Helen Voigt sat at the desk in the bedroom of her apartment in Duluth and wrote a letter.

Not an email. A letter. On paper, in ink, in an envelope with a stamp. She was eighty-nine and she had been writing letters every morning for sixty-one years and she was not going to stop now because her hands shook. Her hands had shaken for five years. The handwriting had gone from precise to legible to a loose scrawl that tilted downhill toward the right margin like water finding the low ground. She could still read it. Whether the recipients could read it was their problem.

The desk was her mother's. Walnut. Small. A writing desk, not a working desk — designed for letters, for one task, for the act of sitting down and putting words on paper and meaning them. It had come north from St. Paul in 1971 when Helen and Walter moved to Duluth for his job at the port authority. The desk had survived four apartments, two houses, one assisted-living facility she'd lasted eleven months in before demanding to leave, and now this — the last apartment, the one on Fourth Street with the view of the lake and the aide who came at eight and the oatmeal she didn't want.

· · ·

The letters went to people. This seems obvious but it had become less obvious as Helen's life narrowed. Eighty-nine years of living had produced an enormous number of people and then, in the way of eighty-nine years, had subtracted most of them. Walter, dead in 1998 — heart, sudden, on a Tuesday in March, shoveling the walk, which Helen had told him not to do and which he had done because Walter's relationship to being told not to do things was to hear the telling, nod, and do them anyway. Their son, David, in Minneapolis, alive, calling every Wednesday, asking the same questions — how are you, did you eat, did the aide come — in a voice that contained love and obligation in proportions Helen could not separate and did not try to. Their daughter, Ruth, in Portland, alive but distant in the way that the West Coast made people distant, not from malice but from weather, from the three-hour time difference that meant Ruth's morning was Helen's noon and the offset accumulated over years into a gentle drift.

But the letters didn't go to David or Ruth. The letters went to people Helen chose each morning based on a principle she had never articulated but which operated with the reliability of a clock. She wrote to people she was thinking of. Not people she should write to, not people who expected to hear from her, not people who would write back. People she was thinking of at seven in the morning when she sat at the desk with the pen and the paper and the light coming through the window from the lake.

Today she was thinking of Marion Hess.

· · ·

Marion Hess had been Helen's neighbor on Piedmont Avenue from 1974 to 1989. Fifteen years. Their houses shared a driveway, which was a source of exactly as much conflict as a shared driveway produces, which was some. Marion's husband, Carl, parked too far to the left. Walter parked too far to the right. The cars did not touch but they communed, they exchanged proximity, and the women negotiated the driveway with the diplomatic care of nations sharing a border.

Helen had not spoken to Marion in twenty years. Marion was in a memory care facility in Roseville. Helen knew this from Marion's daughter, Linda, who had sent a Christmas card in 2019 with a note: Mom is comfortable but doesn't always know who we are. Helen had written back: She knows. She just can't get to it. Linda had not responded to this, which Helen understood. It was the kind of thing that sounded wise from the outside and painful from the inside and Linda was on the inside.

Helen wrote:

Dear Marion,

I'm thinking about the summer Carl painted the garage and got primer on Walter's hydrangeas and Walter didn't say anything for three days and then said everything all at once on a Saturday morning while you and I drank coffee on your porch and listened through the open window. You said: "They needed this." You were right. They were terrible at being angry and wonderful at getting over it. The hydrangeas were fine. They were always going to be fine.

I can see the lake this morning. It's doing the thing where the ice is breaking up but hasn't committed — half-frozen, half-open, the water showing in dark patches like something trying to remember what it looks like. I think about you when I see ice breaking up because of the time you fell on our shared driveway and I ran out and you were on your back laughing and you said, "Helen, the sky is spectacular from down here." You had a bruised tailbone for three weeks and you never complained about it except to say that sitting was overrated.

I don't know if you'll read this. I don't know if Linda reads them to you. It doesn't matter. I'm not writing to the Marion who reads. I'm writing to the Marion who fell on the driveway and looked at the sky. She's in there. She just can't get to it.

Love, Helen.

· · ·

She addressed the envelope. Her handwriting on the envelope was worse than in the letter because the envelope was harder — the surface slick, the pen sliding, no friction to hold the letters in place. Marion's address at the facility was in Helen's address book, a leather-bound thing she'd had since 1978, the entries written and crossed out and rewritten as people moved and married and died, the pages a palimpsest of sixty years of where people were. Some pages had five addresses for one person. Some had one address with a line through it and nothing after, which was its own kind of address.

She put the stamp on. Stamps were a dollar now, which was outrageous when she thought about it and irrelevant when she didn't. She had a roll of a hundred in the desk drawer. David had bought them for her birthday — a hundred stamps, which was a strange gift and also the best gift, because it said: I know you'll use these. I know you'll be here to use them. A hundred mornings. A hundred letters. A hundred stamps between now and whenever the roll ran out.

She put the letter on the small table by the door where the outgoing mail went. The aide, Patience — a Liberian woman of thirty-five whose name, like Patient at the grocery in Burlington, described her professional requirement with the precision of destiny — would take it to the mailbox on her way out. Patience took a letter to the mailbox every day. She had once asked Helen who she was writing to and Helen had said, "Everyone I'm thinking of," and Patience had nodded as if this were a normal answer, which it was, for Helen.

· · ·

The letters had started in 1965. Walter's mother, Ingrid, was dying in a hospital in Mankato — stomach cancer, slow, the kind that let you know it was coming — and Helen had written to her every day for the last three months. Not cards. Letters. About what David did at school. About the weather. About the neighbor's dog that barked at squirrels with a conviction that suggested it believed each squirrel was a new and unique threat. Small things. Things that were not about dying.

Ingrid had kept the letters in the drawer of her hospital nightstand. When she died, the drawer had ninety-one letters in it, rubber-banded, in order. Walter had brought them home. Helen had put them in a box and the box was in the closet of this apartment, behind the winter coats she no longer wore because she no longer went outside in winter because the ice, the ice, the same ice that had put Marion on her back in the driveway — the ice was a threat now instead of a joke and Helen hated this, hated the fear, hated the caution, hated that a thing she'd walked on for eighty winters had become an enemy, but she accepted it because the alternative was a broken hip and a broken hip at eighty-nine was not a broken hip, it was an ending.

After Ingrid, the letters continued. Not daily — she didn't write to the dying every day. But every morning. One letter. To whoever surfaced. The discipline was the sitting down. The practice was the thinking of. The pen touching the paper was the moment where the thinking became real, became a physical object that would travel through the mail — the actual mail, with trucks and sorting machines and a carrier on foot — and arrive in someone's hands and be opened and read or not read and it didn't matter because the letter was not the point. The writing was the point. The thinking of someone was the point.

· · ·

Sometimes she wrote to the dead. She did not tell David or Ruth about this because they would worry, and their worry would have the particular quality of worry about a parent's cognition — is she confused, is she declining, does she know they're dead. She knew they were dead. She wrote to them anyway. She wrote to Walter about the lake. She wrote to her friend Dorothy, dead since 2012, about a book she'd read. She wrote to Ingrid, dead since 1965, about what David had become, the Wednesday calls, the stamps.

These letters she did not mail. She wrote them and put them in the box with Ingrid's ninety-one and the box grew slowly, a few letters a month, the living and the dead accumulating in the same container. This seemed right. The box didn't sort them. The box just held them.

Her hand hurt today. The right hand, the writing hand, the one that had held the pen for sixty-one years and was now informing her, through a deep ache in the knuckle of the index finger, that sixty-one years was a long time to hold anything. She flexed it. She put the pen down. She picked it up. She'd finish the morning. Tomorrow her hand would hurt again and she'd write again because the hurt was the cost and the letter was the thing and the thing was non-negotiable in the way that Lucía's count was non-negotiable and Adama's coins were non-negotiable — a practice so deeply held that stopping it would not be a decision but a disappearance.

Patience arrived at eight. Helen heard the key, the door, the soft announcement — "Good morning, Mrs. Voigt" — and she closed the desk. The letter was on the table. The pen was in the drawer. The morning's thinking-of was done.

"Letter today?" Patience asked.

"Always," Helen said.

Patience took it. The door closed. The letter went into the world — through the mailbox, into the truck, across the miles to Roseville where Marion sat in a chair by a window and did or did not remember the driveway and the sky. It didn't matter. The letter would arrive and it would be an object that had traveled from one person to another through the physical world and it would contain Marion's name in Helen's handwriting and that was enough. That was what a letter was. Not communication. Proof. Proof that at seven in the morning on a Wednesday in February, in an apartment in Duluth with a view of the lake and ice breaking up in dark patches, someone was thinking of you.

Helen sat at the desk for another minute. The lake was doing the thing. The ice was breaking. She was eighty-nine and her hand hurt and the oatmeal was coming and she would eat it and she would be here tomorrow to write another letter and she did not know to whom and that was fine. She'd know at seven. She always knew at seven.
