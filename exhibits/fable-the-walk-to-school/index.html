The Walk to School

Fable · February 2026

Every morning Tomás Herrera walked his daughter Lucía seven blocks to Garfield Elementary, and every morning Lucía stopped at the same crack in the sidewalk on Myrtle Street and would not cross it until she had counted to eleven.

Not ten. Not twelve. Eleven. She was seven and the number was non-negotiable. Tomás had asked once why eleven and Lucía had looked at him as if he'd asked why gravity and said, "Because that's how many it takes." He did not ask again.

The crack was on the third block, between the house with the ceramic frog and the house with the chain-link fence where the three-legged pit bull lived. The pit bull's name was Oso and he came to the fence every morning and pressed his nose through the diamonds and Lucía said hello to him during the count — "one, hi Oso, two, three" — and Oso wagged with his entire back half because a three-legged dog cannot wag without committing to it fully.

Tomás stood and waited during the count. He held her backpack — purple, with an iron-on patch of a capybara that his sister in Puebla had sent — because Lucía said the count didn't work if she was carrying anything. She had to be unburdened. Her hands had to be free. She stood at the crack and counted and her hands hung at her sides and her lips moved and sometimes she closed her eyes and sometimes she didn't and Tomás watched her and held the backpack and did not check his phone.

He had checked his phone once. In September. And Lucía had stopped at six and started over because, she said, "You weren't listening." He had not been listening. He'd been reading a text from his foreman about a change to the pour schedule at the site on Figueroa. Lucía had seen his eyes go away and the count had broken and she'd had to start again and they'd been two minutes late and her teacher, Mrs. Bhandari, had given Tomás a look that contained the entire history of fathers not paying attention.

He did not check his phone anymore.

· · ·

The walk took nineteen minutes including the count. Without the count it would have taken fifteen, but Tomás had stopped calculating the difference because calculating the difference was a way of saying the count was a waste and the count was not a waste. The count was the point. The four minutes were the point. The standing and waiting and holding the backpack and being present for an act of private magic performed by a seven-year-old at a crack in the sidewalk in El Sereno, Los Angeles, every morning between 7:41 and 7:45 — that was the walk.

Tomás was a concrete finisher. He worked for Reyes & Sons, which was not Reyes and not sons but a Guatemalan named Abelardo who'd bought the name from the previous owner and kept it because the trucks were already painted. Tomás finished concrete, which meant he was the last person to touch it — the one who troweled the surface smooth, who edged the joints, who made the thing that would be walked on and driven on and stood on look like it had always been there. He was good at this. He had the hands for it — wide, patient, steady in the way that a surface demands. Concrete doesn't forgive impatience. It sets on its own schedule and you work within that schedule or you ruin it.

His wife, Carmen, had left in March. Not dramatically — no fight, no door, no scene. She'd packed three bags while Lucía was at school and Tomás was at a job site in Alhambra and she'd driven to her cousin's apartment in Riverside and texted him at 4:17 PM: I can't do this anymore. She should stay with you. You're the one who pays attention.

This was true and also devastating and Tomás had sat in his truck in the parking lot of the job site and read the text six times and then driven home and made dinner — rice and chicken thighs with the adobo from the red can, Lucía's favorite — and when Lucía asked where Mama was he said Mama was visiting Tía Daniela and would be gone for a while. This was true in the way that a lie can be true if you say it gently enough. Carmen was at Daniela's. She was gone for a while. The "while" had become six months and then permanent and the paperwork was filed and Lucía had stopped asking because children stop asking when the answer stops changing.

· · ·

The count had started in April, three weeks after Carmen left. Lucía had stopped at the crack — the same crack, always the same crack, the one shaped like a river delta, three lines branching from one — and said, "Wait." And she'd counted to eleven and then crossed and said, "Okay," and Tomás had said, "Okay," and they'd kept walking. The next day she did it again. And the next. And every day since.

Tomás had considered worrying about it. His mother, who called from Puebla every Sunday, said he should worry about it. His sister said it was a phase. Mrs. Bhandari, at the parent-teacher conference in October, had mentioned it carefully — "Lucía has some routines that are important to her" — in the way that teachers mention things when they want you to take them to a professional without saying "take her to a professional."

He had not taken her to a professional. He had considered it. He had looked up the co-pay on his insurance — forty-five dollars, which was not nothing but was manageable — and he had driven past a building on Eastern Avenue that said CHILD AND FAMILY COUNSELING in letters that were meant to be reassuring and were not. He had not gone in. He told himself he would go in if the count changed — if it got longer, if it spread to other cracks, if she started counting at home or at school. But it didn't change. Eleven. One crack. One time per day. The same every morning for seven months now, steady as concrete.

What he hadn't told anyone — not his mother, not his sister, not Mrs. Bhandari — was that the count worked. Not in whatever way Lucía thought it worked — he didn't know what she thought it did, what magic it performed, what door it opened or closed. But it worked on him. The four minutes of standing still. The backpack in his hand. The attention. It was the only time in his day when he was required to do nothing but be present and witness something he didn't understand. The rest of his day was comprehensible — concrete had rules, trowels had angles, joints had depths. The count had no rules he could see. It asked him to stand in mystery and he stood.

· · ·

On a Tuesday in November it rained. Not LA rain — the apologetic mist that didn't commit — but real rain, the kind that came twice a year and made the city confused and beautiful and slightly dangerous. The streets in El Sereno flooded at the low spots because the drains were designed for a city that didn't believe in rain and the water came anyway.

Tomás put Lucía in her rain boots — yellow, with frogs on them, bought at the Target on Atlantic — and they walked. The umbrella was his, large, black, the kind a man buys when he decides to take weather seriously. He held it over Lucía. His left shoulder got wet. This was the arrangement.

At the crack, Lucía stopped. The crack was full of water. The three branches of the delta were running like actual rivers, miniature, carrying leaf fragments and dirt. Lucía looked at the water and looked at Tomás and he could see the problem: the crack was obscured. The ritual required the crack and the crack was underwater.

"It's still there," Tomás said.

"I know."

"Under the water."

"I know."

She stood. Oso was at the fence, wet, wagging. The rain hit the umbrella with the sound of a drum being played by someone who didn't know drums. Lucía's lips moved. One. Two. Three. She counted to eleven with her eyes on the water where the crack was, trusting it was there, and when she finished she stepped over it — a larger step than usual, to clear the puddle — and said, "Okay."

"Okay," Tomás said.

They kept walking. The rain kept falling. The umbrella kept his daughter dry and his shoulder wet and the water ran in the streets and in the cracks and under the ground and the city that didn't believe in rain was getting rained on anyway and somewhere in Riverside Carmen was or was not thinking about a seven-year-old girl counting to eleven at a crack that she couldn't see but knew was there, and Tomás was holding a backpack with a capybara on it and an umbrella over a person he had made and the walk took nineteen minutes and the four minutes were the point and he knew this and he did not check his phone.
