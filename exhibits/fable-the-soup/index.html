The Soup

Fable · February 2026

On the first Thursday of every month, Adama Diallo made superkanja and brought it to the mosque on Broad Street in Burlington, Vermont, and this had been true for eleven years and she did not intend to stop.

Superkanja was okra soup. This was like saying the ocean was water — technically correct and entirely useless. Superkanja was okra and fish and palm oil and dawadawa and smoked shrimp and onion and hot pepper and time. Especially time. The soup took six hours if she started from dried fish, which she did, because dried fish from scratch was the difference between soup and the soup, and Adama had not moved four thousand miles from Bamako to Burlington to make a version of her mother's recipe that was merely soup.

She started at six in the morning. The dried fish — tilapia, bought in bulk from the African grocery in Winooski that was run by a Congolese man named Patient who was patient in exactly the way his name demanded — went into a pot of water to soak. While it soaked she cut the okra. Two pounds. Fresh when she could get it, frozen when she couldn't, and in January in Vermont she couldn't, so the okra came from a bag from the freezer and she did not apologize for this because the alternative was no soup and no soup was not an option.

The cutting was important. Adama's mother, Fatoumata, had cut okra in coins — thin rounds that dissolved in the pot and thickened the broth into something that clung to a spoon. Adama cut coins. She had tried other shapes — in her twenties, when she was still experimenting, still believing she could improve the recipe, she'd tried dicing, had tried long strips, had once in a fit of ambition julienned the okra like a French chef because she was taking a cooking class at the community college and the instructor kept saying julienne as if it were a prayer. The julienned superkanja was a crime. Adama ate it alone and told no one.

Coins. Always coins.

· · ·

Adama was fifty-three. She'd come to Burlington in 2004 with her husband, Moussa, who had a cousin already here working at the Ben & Jerry's factory in St. Albans. Moussa had gotten a job there too — night shift, quality control, tasting ice cream from eleven PM to seven AM, a job that sounded like a joke and was not a joke. He came home smelling like waffle cones and fell asleep at eight in the morning and woke at four in the afternoon and ate whatever Adama had made and went back. This was the rhythm for three years. Then Ben & Jerry's laid off the night shift and Moussa drove a taxi and then Moussa drove for Uber and then Moussa's back gave out and now Moussa sat in the living room of their apartment on North Avenue and watched soccer on his phone and did the exercises the physical therapist had given him and did not complain because Moussa's relationship to suffering was the same as his relationship to cold weather: he acknowledged it existed and then he put on another layer.

They had three children. Ibrahima, twenty-four, who worked at the hospital in the billing department and wore glasses and called every Sunday. Aminata, twenty-one, who was at UVM studying something that changed every semester — biology, then sociology, then "interdisciplinary studies," which Adama understood to mean "I don't know yet" and which she accepted because she hadn't known at twenty-one either; at twenty-one she'd been selling fabric in the Medina market in Bamako and the only thing she'd known was that she didn't want to sell fabric forever. And Ousmane, seventeen, who was quiet and tall and drew in a sketchbook he didn't show anyone and who helped Adama with the soup without being asked, which was the thing that broke her heart in the good way, the way that hearts are meant to break — not into pieces but into more room.

· · ·

Ousmane was there this Thursday. He'd come to the kitchen at six-thirty, still in the sweatpants he slept in, and started peeling the onions without a word. He peeled onions the way he did everything — slowly, carefully, with an attention that looked like indifference but wasn't. He was the only one of her children who cooked with her. Ibrahima was too impatient. Aminata was too busy. Ousmane was neither. He was present in the kitchen the way he was present in the world — quietly, with his hands.

"Did you eat?" Adama asked.

"Not yet."

"Eat first."

"I'll eat later."

"Eat now. You can't cook hungry."

"You cook hungry every time."

This was true. Adama cooked the soup on an empty stomach because the soup needed her full attention and eating divided attention and also because the hunger sharpened her palate. She could taste the soup better hungry. She could feel the balance — the salt, the smoke, the heat, the green — with a precision that a full stomach smudged. This was not something she could explain to a doctor or a nutritionist. It was something she knew the way she knew to cut in coins.

Ousmane ate a banana and came back and they worked in silence. The fish was soaking. The onions were peeled. Adama heated the palm oil — red, thick, the color of sunset in a bottle — and started the base. Onion in first. Low heat. The sizzle was wrong if it was loud. A loud sizzle meant the oil was too hot and the onions would burn and burned onions were the one mistake you couldn't fix. You could fix too much salt. You could fix too little pepper. You could not unburn an onion. It went into the soup's memory and stayed there, a bitterness under everything, like a lie told early in a conversation that colors everything that follows.

The onions softened. Adama added the dawadawa — fermented locust beans, black and pungent, smelling like something between cheese and earth. Ousmane made a face. He always made a face. The dawadawa smell was the smell of the soup's ancestry, its connection to a place neither of them lived anymore, a flavor that existed in Burlington only because Adama brought it here in a jar she'd bought from Patient's store and before that from a warehouse in New Jersey and before that from a woman in a market in Ghana or Burkina Faso or Mali, the supply chain of a flavor that had traveled as far as Adama had and for the same reason: because someone needed it to be here.

· · ·

By ten o'clock the soup was in its middle phase. The fish had been drained and added. The okra was in, softening, releasing its mucilage — the thick, slippery substance that made okra okra, that made some people say they hated okra, that was the entire structural point. Adama stirred. The spoon moved through the pot with increasing resistance as the okra did its work, thickening the broth from water into something else, something that held the fish and the spice in suspension, something that coated the tongue and stayed.

She tasted. Too much salt. No — enough salt, not enough acid. She squeezed half a lime. Tasted again. Better. The lime didn't taste like lime in the soup — it tasted like everything else in the soup but sharper, more itself. This was what acid did. It didn't add flavor. It revealed flavor. It made the existing things more visible, the way cleaning a window doesn't change the view but changes your ability to see it.

Ousmane was drawing at the kitchen table. His sketchbook was open and he was drawing the pot. Not the whole kitchen, not Adama, not the room — just the pot, in pencil, with a level of detail that made Adama stop stirring and look. He'd drawn the steam. Not as a line but as a texture — a hatching that got lighter as it rose, that dissolved into the white of the page, that said steam without drawing steam. He'd drawn the spoon handle sticking out at an angle. He'd drawn the lip of the pot where the soup had dripped and dried in a line that Adama had wiped three times and that kept coming back because the soup was generous and overflowed.

"Can I see?" she asked.

He turned the book toward her. It was the pot, yes, but it was also the morning. It was the six o'clock start and the soaking fish and the coins of okra and the red oil and the dawadawa and the silence between them and the not-eating and the years of Thursdays and the four thousand miles and the mother who cut coins in a kitchen in Bamako and the son who drew the pot in a kitchen in Burlington. It was all of that in a pencil drawing of a pot.

"It's good," she said. Which meant: I see you, and you see me, and the seeing is the soup.

"Thanks," he said. Which meant the same thing.

· · ·

At noon the soup was done. Adama tasted it a final time — the deep, layered, slow taste that six hours produced, the taste that no shortcut could replicate because the shortcut was what the six hours prevented. She ladled it into the two large pots she used for transport — aluminum, dented, bought at the restaurant supply on Williston Road — and Ousmane carried them to the car, a 2014 Camry with a check-engine light that had been on for two years and that Adama had decided was decorative.

The mosque on Broad Street was a converted house. White clapboard, green trim, a sign in Arabic and English. The community had bought it in 2011 with money pooled from thirty families, most of them Somali and Somali Bantu, some Sudanese, some West African like Adama. It was too small for Friday prayers — people spilled onto the porch and into the yard — but on Thursdays it was the right size for the soup.

The Thursday lunch had been Adama's idea. Not a program, not a ministry, not a thing with a name or a budget. Just: she made soup and brought it and people ate. Some of them were from the mosque. Some were from the neighborhood. Some were students from UVM who'd heard there was free food and showed up with the shameless practicality of the young and hungry. Adama fed everyone. The soup was not conditional. You did not need to pray, or believe, or belong, or explain. You needed a bowl.

She set up in the kitchen. Ousmane ladled. Adama watched. The soup went into bowls and the bowls went to hands and the hands carried the bowls to tables and chairs and the porch railing and the front step. An old Somali man named Abdi, who came every Thursday and sat in the same chair and said nothing and ate two bowls and left, was already in his chair. A UVM student in a Patagonia jacket that cost more than Adama's monthly grocery budget was balancing a bowl and a phone and a look of genuine wonder, as if she'd never tasted anything before, which was possibly true in the way that mattered.

Adama stood in the kitchen doorway and watched people eat the soup she'd made from her mother's recipe in her mother's way with her mother's coins in a city that was four thousand miles and thirty years from the kitchen where she'd learned it, and the soup was the same. Not similar. Not adapted. Not a "version." The same. The dawadawa was the same. The okra was the same. The six hours were the same. The soup had traveled with her the way language travels — not in a suitcase but in the body, in the hands, in the knowledge of when the sizzle is too loud and when the lime is enough.

Ousmane was drawing again. A quick sketch on a napkin — the old man in his chair, the bowl in his hands, the steam rising. He'd remember this. He'd remember the Thursdays the way Adama remembered her mother's kitchen — not as a memory but as a practice, a thing his body knew, the coins and the oil and the six hours and the carrying and the feeding and the standing in the doorway watching people eat what you made.

The soup was the same. That was the miracle. Four thousand miles and thirty years and a January in Vermont and frozen okra from a bag and the soup was the same because Adama was the same — not unchanged, not unweathered, but continuous. A line from Bamako to Burlington that had not broken. A recipe that had not been lost. A Thursday that kept happening because she kept making it happen, every month, six hours, two pots, the dented aluminum, the Camry with its decorative check-engine light, the son who helped without being asked.

She washed the pots. She drove home. She'd do it again in four weeks.
